# Phase 6 — Geographically targeted advertising on Meta, Spotify and TikTok

Draft plan, 2026-09-10. Election day is **26 October 2026 — 46 days out.**

Everything below is either verified against a primary source, with the URL inline, or explicitly
flagged as unverified. §6 lists every gap. **This is not legal advice**; §2 quotes the statute
directly and the places where its application to an ad platform is genuinely unsettled are marked.

---

## 1. Which platforms will actually take the ads

Read this section before anything else, because two thirds of the request does not survive it.

**Of the three platforms named, only Meta will sell a Middlesex Centre mayoral campaign an ad.**
TikTok prohibits paid political advertising globally and the prohibition reaches paid creator posts
as well. Spotify does sell political ads — the common belief that it stopped is out of date — but
only in five countries, and Canada is not one of them.

**Budget allocation that follows: Meta 100%, Spotify 0%, TikTok 0%.** A plan that quietly assumed
TikTok would work would have spent real money and three weeks of a 46-day campaign discovering the
first sentence of TikTok's own policy page.

### 1.1 TikTok — will not take the ads. This is a prohibition, not a targeting problem.

TikTok's own advertising policy page, *Politics, Governments, and Elections*
([ads.tiktok.com, last updated July 2026](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-politics-government-and-elections)),
says it in one sentence:

> "We do not allow paid political advertising across any of our monetization features."

The prohibition is drawn wide enough to catch every shape a mayoral campaign could take. The policy
bars ads "referencing, promoting, or opposing ... current candidates or nominees for executive,
legislative, or judicial public offices" — a candidate for head of council in Middlesex Centre is
squarely inside that. It also bars appeals for votes and promotion of electoral participation by
anyone other than the election authority: *"Only official entities responsible for overseeing
elections and referendums are eligible to advertise about upcoming or current elections or
referendums."* In our case that is the Middlesex Centre clerk, not the campaign.

The one carve-out — government and intergovernmental entities working with a TikTok sales rep — is
for tourism, cultural initiatives, public-interest matters and recruitment, and requires a
certification process. It is not a route a candidate can use.

The ban reaches past the ad account. TikTok's
[Branded Content Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/bc-policy/en) prohibits branded
content that mentions, endorses or disparages a political party, political figure or candidate — so
**paying a local creator to post about the campaign is prohibited by the same rule set**, and is the
form the workaround usually takes. There is no version of "spend money on TikTok" that is compliant.

**Verdict: budget zero. Build nothing.** Organic posting from a campaign account is a separate
question and outside the scope of this document, but it is not advertising and the app should not
pretend it is.

### 1.2 Spotify — takes political ads, but not in Canada.

Spotify is the one I would have guessed wrong. It banned political advertising in 2020
([CNN, 27 December 2019](https://www.cnn.com/2019/12/27/media/spotify-political-ads/index.html)),
citing an inability to responsibly review the content — but it **un-banned it in 2022**
([Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/pro/spotify-political-ads-return/)). So "Spotify stopped
accepting political ads" is out of date, and a plan built on that belief would be wrong for the
wrong reason.

The reason Spotify still does not work for us is narrower and harder. Spotify's own
[Advertising Policies](https://adshelp.spotify.com/HelpCenter/s/article/advertising-policies-US)
(verified 2026-09-10) state:

> "At this time, we only permit advertisers to promote political ads in the United States, UK,
> Australia, India, and Japan."

**Canada is not on that list.** A Middlesex Centre mayoral ad is a political ad by Spotify's own
definition — the policy names "ads sponsored by candidates" and "ads that feature a candidate for
elected office" as the category — and the category is not sold in this country. Eligibility also
requires the account holder to complete "an advertiser identity verification process," and secondary
reporting consistently says political inventory is **direct-sales only, not purchasable through the
self-serve Ad Studio** ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/spotify-meta-update-political-ad-updates-2022-385419)).
I could not confirm the direct-sales-only restriction on Spotify's own policy page — the page states
the verification requirement but not the purchase channel — so treat that detail as **unverified**.
It does not change the conclusion: the market restriction alone is dispositive.

**Verdict: budget zero.** The only honest next step, if someone wants to push, is to write to a
Spotify Canadian sales rep and ask whether a Canadian municipal candidate can be onboarded as an
exception. I would not plan around a yes, and with 46 days on the clock I would not spend the week
waiting for the answer.

### 1.3 Meta (Facebook / Instagram) — yes, and it is the whole programme.

Meta sells political advertising in Canada and has done since 2019. Its
[Advertising Standards on Ads about Social Issues, Elections or Politics](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/SIEP-advertising/SIEP)
(last updated 1 July 2026) requires that any advertiser "located in or targeting people in
designated countries must complete the authorization process," and that
*"advertisers must include a verified 'Paid for by' disclaimer on these ads."* The one blanket
territorial exclusion in that policy is the **European Union**, where Meta stopped selling political
ads in response to the TTPA. Canada is not excluded.

**Our ads are in scope whether we like it or not.** The category covers ads "made by, on behalf of or
about a candidate for public office" — it does not carve out municipal offices, and running a
mayoral ad without authorization gets it rejected and the account flagged, not quietly permitted.

**Authorization: budget three weeks, and start it the day this plan is approved.** The process is
identity verification (government-issued photo ID), two-factor on the account, and — the slow step —
a **physical letter with a confirmation code mailed to a residential address**, typically 3–10
business days ([ACM Strategies](https://www.acmstrategies.com/post/how-to-get-authorized-to-run-ads-about-social-issues-elections-or-politics-on-meta),
[Surge Tactic](https://www.surgetactic.com/blog/how-to-get-authorized-to-run-social-issue-or-political-facebook-ads)).
Then a disclaimer review of roughly a day or two. Practitioner guides converge on **two to three
weeks end to end**. I could not retrieve Meta's own Business Help Center pages for this — they render
as JavaScript shells to a fetcher and I have no logged-in session — so **the mailed-code detail and
the timings are secondary sources, not Meta's own words**. Treat them as planning assumptions, not
facts, and verify by starting the flow.

**The municipal-candidate question — the one I most expected to be a blocker — is not one.** Meta's
disclaimer machinery lets an advertiser name *themselves*, a Page they run, or an organization as the
"Paid for by" entity; naming an organization asks for a phone number, email and website. There is no
mandatory national registration number, no party ID, no election-authority filing number. **A private
individual with a driver's licence and a mailing address can complete this.** That matters, because
it is exactly the step where Google's political-ads verification has historically demanded things a
municipal campaign does not have — see §6.

With 46 days left, the sequencing point is blunt: **the mailed letter is on the critical path, and
nothing in §4 or §5 is worth building until it has arrived.**

#### 1.3.1 Targeting granularity — and a widely-repeated claim that is wrong

This is where I expected to write the bad news, and it turns out the bad news is not true.

Search the web for Meta political ad targeting and you will be told, repeatedly and confidently, that
political ads are a **Special Ad Category** and therefore carry a **15-mile / 25 km minimum radius
and no postal-code targeting** — see for instance
[Data Axle](https://www.data-axle.com/resources/blog/meta-special-ad-categories-rules/) and a dozen
agency blogs saying the same thing. If that were true it would end the project: a 25 km circle
anywhere in Middlesex Centre swallows most of London.

**Meta's own developer documentation says otherwise.** The
[Special Ad Category reference](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/audiences/special-ad-category/)
sets out the geographic restriction — *"Location selection must include all areas equal or larger
than 15 mile or 25 kilometer radius for the US and Canada"*, and a prohibition on `zips`,
`neighborhood`, `electoral_district` and other sub-city types — and scopes it to `HOUSING`,
`EMPLOYMENT` and `FINANCIAL_PRODUCTS_SERVICES`. On `ISSUES_ELECTIONS_POLITICS` it states plainly:

> "Audience options for ads about social issues, elections, or politics are not affected by the
> `special_ad_categories` label."

So political ads keep **full geographic targeting**: pin-drop radius down to the ordinary minimum of
**1 mile / ~1.6 km**, and postal-code targeting. The 15-mile rule is an anti-discrimination measure
aimed at housing, jobs and credit, and the blogosphere has generalised it to a category it does not
touch.

**I want to be honest about the confidence here.** This is Meta's own documentation and it is
unambiguous, but it is the *Marketing API* documentation rather than the Ads Manager help centre, and
API and UI have diverged before. **Verify it in Ads Manager before building anything on it**: create a
draft political ad set, drop a pin on Ilderton, and see whether the radius slider will go below 25 km.
That check costs ten minutes and settles the single most important design input in this document. If
the slider *does* floor at 25 km, §3 collapses to postal-code targeting alone — which still works,
but the whole cost model changes.

On postal codes, Meta accepts Canadian codes in the location field. Secondary sources say both
three-character FSAs and full six-character codes are accepted
([Keepers Digital](https://keepersdigital.com/en-ca/resources/paid-social/meta-local-targeting-radius/));
**I could only confirm that Canadian postal codes are supported at all, not that six-character
precision is honoured**, and six-character precision in rural Ontario would in any case run straight
into Meta's minimum-audience floor. Plan on **FSA level**, and see §3 for why that is nearly ideal here.

## 2. What Ontario law requires

Three separate regimes touch a campaign ad: what must appear *on* it, what it *costs against the
limit*, and the entirely separate rules for someone who is **not** the campaign. Plus the constraint
this repo already lives under, which advertising must not quietly breach.

### 2.1 The ad itself — MEA s. 88.3

The *Municipal Elections Act, 1996*
([e-Laws consolidation](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/96m32)) defines the thing at s. 88.3(1):

> "'election campaign advertisement' means an advertisement in any broadcast, print, electronic or
> other medium that has the purpose of promoting or supporting the election of a candidate."

"Electronic or other medium" is doing the work — a Facebook ad is squarely inside it. Two obligations
follow, and they are less onerous than people expect:

- **s. 88.3(2):** *"An election campaign advertisement purchased by or under the direction of a
  candidate shall identify the candidate."* That is the whole on-ad requirement. Ontario does **not**
  prescribe federal-style "Authorized by the official agent for…" wording for municipal campaigns.
  An ad that says *Sean Hunt for Mayor of Middlesex Centre* satisfies s. 88.3(2) on its face.
- **s. 88.3(3):** before causing the ad to appear, the candidate must give the **broadcaster or
  publisher, in writing**: (1) the name of the candidate, and (2) *"the name, business address and
  telephone number of the individual who deals with the broadcaster or publisher under the direction
  of the candidate."*

**s. 88.3(3) is the interesting one and I am not certain how it lands on Meta.** Meta is plainly the
publisher. The authorization flow does collect a verified identity and a disclaimer entity with phone,
email and address — which is substantially the information s. 88.3(3) demands, provided in writing,
before the ad runs. Whether a regulator or a compliance auditor would call that "provided to the
publisher in writing" is a question for counsel, not for me. **The cheap defensive answer is to do it
belt-and-braces**: complete Meta's authorization *and* keep a dated written record — a PDF or an
email to the campaign's own file — stating the candidate's name and the ad contact's name, business
address and telephone number for each platform used. It costs nothing and it is the artefact you
would want to hand a compliance audit committee under s. 88.33.

Note also **s. 88.3(5)**: the publisher must keep the ad, the s. 88.3(3) information, and a statement
of the charge, for **four years**, open to public inspection. Meta's Ad Library retains political ads
for **seven years**, which over-satisfies this — but the retention duty is the *publisher's*, so it is
not a substitute for the campaign's own records under s. 88.25.

### 2.2 Spending — s. 88.19, s. 88.20 and O. Reg. 101/97

Ad spend counts. **s. 88.19(3) para. 10** makes "the cost of election campaign advertisements (within
the meaning of section 88.3)" an expense, explicitly. **s. 88.20(6)** caps expenses incurred between
nomination and voting day by the prescribed formula, and **s. 88.20(8)** exempts paragraphs 3 and 5–9
of s. 88.19(3) — audit fees, fundraising, post-election parties, recounts, compliance audits,
disability-related costs. **Paragraph 10 is not exempt. Advertising is inside the cap, at full value.**

The formula is [O. Reg. 101/97](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/970101) s. 5 para. 1:

> "In the case of a candidate for the office of head of council of a municipality, the amount shall be
> calculated by adding together **$7,500 plus 85 cents for each elector** entitled to vote for the
> office."

At the **16,892 electors** this database holds, that is **$7,500 + $14,358.20 = $21,858.20** — the same
figure `docs/phase-5-messaging-plan.md` uses, which is a good sign the two documents agree. The
authoritative number is the clerk's certified elector count, not ours; the app should treat the limit
as an **operator-entered figure with our count offered as a default**, never as something it computes
and asserts.

Two further points that decide how the app should model spend:

- **s. 88.20(2) and (5):** an expense may only be incurred by a candidate or someone acting under the
  candidate's direction, and only inside the campaign period. So the *date the charge is incurred*
  matters, not the date the invoice is paid — which means a campaign object in the app needs a
  `spend_incurred_on` notion, not just a total.
- **Value, not cash.** s. 88.19(3) para. 2 makes "the value of contributions of goods and services" an
  expense. **A volunteer's or donor's ad account paying for a campaign ad is still a campaign expense
  at its value**, and is also a contribution. There is no free ad spend. If the app tracks spend, it
  has to be able to record a spend line whose payer is not the campaign account.

### 2.3 Third parties — a regime the campaign must stay out of, not use

ss. 88.4–88.6 create a separate registration system for advertising by anyone who is not the
candidate. **s. 88.4(1)**: no individual, corporation or trade union may incur expenses for a third
party advertisement during the restricted period unless registered under s. 88.6. Registered third
parties carry their own on-ad requirements — **s. 88.5(1)** requires the ad to state the registered
third party's name, the municipality where it is registered, and a phone number, mailing address or
email — and their own limit: O. Reg. 101/97 s. 7 gives **$5,000 plus five cents per elector, to a
maximum of $25,000**, which for Middlesex Centre is **$5,844.60**.

The rule that matters to *us* is **s. 88.6(5) para. 1: a candidate whose nomination has been filed is
not eligible to register as a third party.** There is no double-dipping and no second budget. And an
enthusiastic supporter who buys their own Facebook ads for Sean Hunt is either (a) acting "under the
direction of" the candidate — in which case it is a campaign expense against our $21,858.20, and must
be recorded — or (b) acting independently — in which case **they must register with the clerk before
spending a dollar**, and their ads need their own s. 88.5 identification. Both routes have paperwork.
Neither route is "a friend just boosts a post."

This is worth a line in the app: if the campaign ever records a spend line it did not itself pay for,
that is a flag for a human, not a checkbox.

### 2.4 The voters list must not leak through the ad channel

Nothing above displaces the constraint the whole repo is built around. As `CLAUDE.md` and
`docs/data-sources-research.md` set out, the list is governed by **MEA s. 23(7)–(8)**: it may be used
only for electoral purposes, and a downstream recipient *"shall not provide it to any other person,
and shall not make further copies, either in printed form or electronically."*

Uploading elector names, addresses, emails or phone numbers to Meta as a Custom Audience is exactly
that — providing the list to another person and causing further electronic copies to be made, on
infrastructure that will never give us the s. 23(8) para. 1(iii) written acknowledgment of
destruction that a candidate is required to file and retain. **So: no Custom Audiences, no Lookalikes
built from list data, no hashed-email or hashed-phone uploads, ever.** Hashing does not help; the
statute restricts providing and copying, not readable text.

That single constraint is what makes this a **geography** feature rather than an audience feature,
and §3 and §4 are written accordingly.

## 3. Targeting rural Middlesex Centre without paying for London

Everything below was computed from the loaded database (`data/households.csv`, 7,140 households /
16,892 electors, 7,067 with coordinates) and `data/mc_boundary.json`, on 2026-09-10. These are our
own numbers, not estimates.

### 3.1 The problem, stated as arithmetic

Middlesex Centre had a **2021 census population of 18,928**. The City of London, which it wraps
around on three sides, had **422,324**
([CBC on the 2021 census](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/london/london-ontario-population-2021-census-1.6344869)).
Middlesex Centre's own centroid sits about **12 km from London City Hall**, and its southern and
eastern edges touch the city limit.

So the ratio to hold in mind is roughly **22 Londoners for every Middlesex Centre resident**, and
Londoners cannot vote in this election. Any targeting mistake does not cost 10% waste — it costs
90%-plus, because the moment a shape crosses the city line it lands in a population an order of
magnitude larger and much denser than ours.

I ran four candidate targeting plans against the real household coordinates. "Area inside" is the
share of the targeted *land area* that falls within the municipal boundary, by Monte Carlo sampling
against `mc_boundary.json`:

| Plan | Electors reached | Area inside Middlesex Centre |
|---|---|---|
| A — five pins, 3–5 km radii | 12,599 (75.1%) | 89.5% |
| B — five pins, 8 km radii | 14,979 (89.3%) | 63.3% |
| C — one pin, 25 km (the radius floor *if* the Special Ad Category rule applied) | 16,781 (100%) | 30.2% |
| D — five pins, 25 km | 16,781 (100%) | 17.5% |

**Read the last column as a floor on the waste, not a measure of it.** Area is not people. Plan C's
70% of "outside" area contains the entire City of London; plan A's 10.5% of outside area is farmland
and the fringes of Lucan and Mount Brydges. **A plan that is 30% inside by area is closer to 4%
inside by population.** That is the shape of the risk: the budget does not degrade gracefully as the
circles grow, it falls off a cliff the moment London is inside one.

This is also why §1.3.1 matters so much. If the 25 km Special Ad Category floor genuinely applied to
political ads, the only honest advice in this document would be *do not buy Meta ads*, because plan C
is the best you could do and plan C is mostly a London ad buy. Meta's own API documentation says it
does not apply. **Verify that in Ads Manager before spending anything.**

### 3.2 The approach I would actually take: postal codes first, circles second

Here is the fact that makes this tractable, and it is a piece of luck rather than cleverness.
Middlesex Centre's households fall almost entirely into two rural FSAs, and **London's FSAs are
disjoint from them**. London is `N5*` and `N6*`; Middlesex Centre is `N0L` and `N0M`:

| FSA | Households | Electors | Share of all electors |
|---|---|---|---|
| **N0L** (Komoka, Kilworth, Delaware) | 3,306 | 8,008 | 47.4% |
| **N0M** (Ilderton, Arva, Denfield, Coldstream) | 3,263 | 7,621 | 45.1% |
| N6P / N6H / N5X / N7G and other London-delivery codes | 509 | 1,180 | 7.0% |
| blank / unmapped | 62 | 83 | 0.5% |

**Buying `N0L` + `N0M` reaches 15,629 of 16,892 electors — 92.5% — and cannot, by construction,
serve a single ad to a London postal address.** That is a far better waste profile than any circle
plan, and it is one line of targeting configuration.

The waste that remains is real but benign: `N0L` and `N0M` extend past the municipal boundary into
Strathroy-Caradoc, Lucan Biddulph, North Middlesex, Thames Centre and Southwold — rural neighbours
with a fraction of London's population. **I could not verify how much**: I do not have FSA population
totals or FSA boundary polygons to hand, and I am not going to guess at a number that determines the
budget. **Action: pull StatCan's 2021 Census FSA-level profile and the FSA cartographic boundary
file** (both free), intersect the two FSAs with `mc_boundary.json` — the app already owns the
point-in-polygon code in `api/src/lib/geo.ts` — and you will have the exact "electors per dollar"
ratio instead of my hand-wave. That is a half-day of work and it is the single most valuable number
in this plan.

**The 7% in London-delivery FSAs is a trap, and the answer is not to buy them.** Those 1,180 electors
live in Middlesex Centre but get their mail through London stations — N5X is north London
(Masonville, Stoneybrook), N6H is west London, N6P is Lambeth. Adding `N5X` to the buy to reach ~164
electors would put the ad in front of roughly forty thousand Londoners. **Do not.** Reach those doors
the way the rest of this app already reaches them: canvassing, lawn signs, and the turf sheet. If
they must be reached digitally, that is what a **tight pin-drop circle** is for — 3 km on Arva, 3 km
on the Delaware/Lambeth fringe — which is plan A's geometry and stays 89.5% inside the boundary.

So the recommended shape is:

1. **One ad set, postal codes `N0L` + `N0M`.** 92.5% of electors, zero London exposure. This is the
   default, and for a first buy it may be the only ad set.
2. **Optionally, a second small ad set of tight circles** (≤ 5 km) on Arva, Komoka and Delaware, with
   its own small budget, to pick up the fringe — but only after checking §1.3.1's radius floor, and
   only with an explicit exclusion of the London postal codes if Meta permits location exclusion on
   this category.
3. **Never a single wide circle.** Plan C and plan D are the two shapes an inexperienced hand reaches
   for, and both are mostly a London ad buy.

### 3.3 Two constraints that will bite in practice

**Minimum audience size.** Meta will not deliver to an ad set whose estimated audience is below a
floor. In rural Ontario a single six-character postal code, or a 1.6 km circle over Denfield, will
sit under it. This is another argument for FSA-level targeting: `N0L` and `N0M` are comfortably large,
where a single village is not. **I could not find Meta's published numeric floor** and would not
quote one; you will discover it as an unspendable ad set in Ads Manager.

**Small radii deliver badly in low-density geography.** Practitioner reporting is consistent that
sub-5-mile radii in sparse areas get throttled delivery and inflated CPMs
([Thread Transfer](https://thread-transfer.com/blog/2026-06-17-meta-ads-location-targeting-radius-minimum/)).
Unverified, and the sources are agency blogs rather than Meta — but it is directionally plausible
(fewer people, thinner auction) and it points the same way as everything else in this section:
**FSA targeting is the primary instrument; circles are a supplement, not the plan.**

### 3.4 Where the app's own data earns its keep

The per-community geometry the app can already compute, and which a human would otherwise eyeball
badly:

| Community | Households | Electors | Centroid | r₉₀ (km) | To London City Hall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Komoka / Kilworth | 2,613 | 6,331 | 42.9576, −81.4209 | 3.4 | 14.6 km |
| Ilderton | 2,211 | 5,172 | 43.0677, −81.3936 | 11.9 | 15.2 km |
| Delaware | 653 | 1,588 | 42.9093, −81.4116 | 1.7 | 15.9 km |
| Denfield | 575 | 1,361 | 43.1139, −81.3977 | 14.4 | 19.0 km |
| London-delivery (in-municipality) | 460 | 1,067 | 42.9702, −81.3598 | 15.2 | 9.5 km |
| Arva | 436 | 1,006 | 43.0561, −81.2872 | 6.9 | 8.6 km |

r₉₀ is the radius from the centroid containing 90% of that community's households. Note how it
separates the two kinds of place: **Delaware and Komoka are villages** (r₉₀ of 1.7 and 3.4 km — a
tight circle works), while **Ilderton and Denfield are postal delivery areas covering hundreds of
square kilometres of farmland** (r₉₀ of 11.9 and 14.4 km — a circle that covers them covers half the
county). Anyone drawing circles on a map by name, without this table, would draw the wrong ones.

Note also Arva: centroid **8.6 km from London City Hall**, r₉₀ of 6.9 km. Arva's circle and London
overlap at almost any useful radius. Arva is a canvassing problem, not an advertising one.

## 4. What the app would actually build — and what it would not

The temptation with a feature like this is to build an ad platform. Don't. Meta already has one, it
is free, and it is better than anything we would write. **The app's job is the part Meta cannot do:
know where our electors are, and know what the campaign has spent against a statutory limit.**

### 4.1 The line

**In the app** — because it needs our data, or produces a record we are legally obliged to keep:

- **Compute the target geography.** Take the household coordinates and ward/community assignments
  that already exist and emit a *proposal*: the FSA list with elector counts (§3.2), a set of
  candidate pins with radii and their r₉₀ coverage (§3.4), the share of each shape's area inside
  `mc_boundary.json`, and the estimated electors reached. This is the reachability report's shape,
  applied to geography rather than contactability — and `api/src/lib/geo.ts` already has the maths.
- **Cost a proposal.** Enter a budget and a CPM assumption, get impressions and a cost per *elector*
  reached — which is the only unit that matters and the one no ad manager will ever show you,
  because Meta does not know which of its users can vote here.
- **Record what was actually run and what it cost.** One row per ad set: platform, dates, targeting
  as configured, amount incurred, date incurred, who authorised it, and the disclaimer text used.
- **Track spend against the MEA limit.** A running total of advertising against the
  operator-entered limit (default $21,858.20, §2.2), showing the date each expense was *incurred*
  rather than paid, and flagging any spend line whose payer is not the campaign account (§2.3).
- **Generate the s. 88.3 paperwork.** The on-ad identification string, and the written s. 88.3(3)
  record — candidate name plus the ad contact's name, business address and telephone number — as a
  dated artefact per platform, filed where a compliance audit under s. 88.33 can find it.

**In Meta's Ads Manager, not here** — because building it would be worse and slower:

- Creative: images, video, copy, A/B variants.
- Bidding, budget pacing, optimisation objective, placements.
- Payment.
- Delivery reporting (impressions, reach, frequency, CPM). Pull the totals in by hand at the end of
  a flight and type them into the spend record. **No Marketing API integration in this phase** — it
  needs an app review, a system user, token rotation and error handling, for a campaign that will
  run perhaps six ad sets in its entire life.
- The Ad Library entry. Meta keeps it for seven years; we do not need a copy.

### 4.2 The hard rule: geography only, never an audience

**No Custom Audiences. No Lookalikes. No uploads of names, addresses, emails or phone numbers to any
ad platform, hashed or not.** MEA s. 23(8) para. 1(i) forbids a downstream recipient from providing
the list onward or making further copies of it electronically, and s. 23(8) para. 1(iii) requires a
written acknowledgment of destruction that Meta will never provide — so an audience upload is a
breach that also leaves an unfileable gap in the s. 23(8) para. 5 records the candidate must retain
until 2030.

Design consequence, and it is a real one: **the API must never expose an endpoint that emits elector
identifiers in an advertising context.** Everything §4.1 describes is *counts and coordinates
aggregated to a shape* — the same discipline `api/src/lib/advice.ts` already enforces for the
Anthropic call, and the same one `serialize.ts` enforces for every row. If a future increment wants
"upload our supporters as a seed audience", the answer is no, and this paragraph is why.

Doorstep phone and email in `voter_contact` are *not* list data and have their own CASL-governed
consent regime — but they were collected for GOTV and campaign updates, **not** for ad targeting, and
per-purpose consent means what it says. They do not go to Meta either.

### 4.3 Schema sketch (migration 005)

```
ad_campaign     id, name, platform ('meta'), objective, status ('proposed'|'authorized'|'running'
                |'complete'|'cancelled'), starts_on, ends_on, budget_cents, disclaimer_text,
                created_by, created_at
ad_target       id, campaign_id, kind ('postal'|'radius'), postal_codes text[],
                centre_lat, centre_lon, radius_km, est_electors, est_households,
                area_inside_pct, computed_at
ad_spend        id, campaign_id, incurred_on, amount_cents, payer ('campaign'|'other'),
                payer_note, invoice_ref, impressions, reach, entered_by, entered_at
```

`ad_target` stores the *computed* estimates alongside the shape so the proposal is reproducible after
the fact — a compliance audit asks what you targeted and why, and "we recomputed it today and got a
different number" is a bad answer. `ad_spend.payer` exists solely to make §2.3's contribution problem
visible. New audit actions: `view_ad_proposal`, `create_ad_campaign`, `update_ad_campaign`,
`record_ad_spend` — added to the `AuditAction` union in `api/src/lib/audit.ts`, never as loose
strings.

Everything here is organizer-and-above. A volunteer has no business seeing the ad budget, and the
geography endpoints read the whole municipality, which `api/src/lib/scope.ts` already treats as an
organizer-or-above operation.

## 5. A phased build

Effort figures are for one developer who knows this codebase.

**Step 0 — start the Meta authorization today. Zero development effort, three weeks of calendar.**
Nothing below is worth building if the account is not authorized, and the mailed confirmation code is
the longest lead time in this document (§1.3). With 46 days to voting day, a week of delay here is a
week of the ad flight, not a week of slack. Do this before reading further.

**Step 1 — the targeting proposal, read-only. ~2 days.**
`GET /api/ads/geography`, organizer-only, audited as `view_ad_proposal`: returns the FSA table from
§3.2 and the per-community centroid/r₉₀ table from §3.4, computed live from `household`, with each
shape's area-inside-boundary share. A single page under `web/src/reports/`. **This alone is worth
shipping even if nothing else is** — it is the difference between buying `N0L`+`N0M` and buying a
25 km circle over London, which is the difference between the budget working and the budget
evaporating. It also directly answers the question the candidate actually has.

**Step 2 — the StatCan FSA check. ~half a day, and do it before spending money.**
Pull the 2021 FSA boundary file and FSA population profile, intersect `N0L` and `N0M` with
`mc_boundary.json`, and produce the honest "what fraction of this FSA can vote for us" ratio (§3.2).
This converts the plan's one big unknown into a number. It is offline analysis, not a feature.

**Step 3 — campaign and spend records. ~2 days.**
Migration 005, `ad_campaign` / `ad_target` / `ad_spend`, a form to record a flight and its spend, and
a running total against the operator-entered MEA limit with the incurred-date semantics of §2.2. The
compliance value is immediate and independent of whether any ad ever runs.

**Step 4 — costing and the s. 88.3 artefacts. ~1 day.**
Budget → impressions → cost-per-elector-reached on top of step 1's numbers; and the generated
identification string plus the dated s. 88.3(3) written record per platform.

**Step 5 — nothing.** Stop here for this election.

### What I would not build

- **No Marketing API integration.** Six ad sets do not justify app review, a system user and token
  rotation. Type the numbers in.
- **No TikTok anything.** §1.1. Not a stub, not a disabled toggle, not a "coming soon" — a switch in
  the UI for a platform that prohibits our ads is an invitation to someone to try.
- **No Spotify anything** unless a Canadian sales rep puts an exception in writing (§1.2).
- **No creative tooling, no image editor, no copy generator.** Meta's tools are free and better.
- **No audience upload of any kind, ever.** §4.2.
- **No automated bid or budget management.** The whole ad budget is under $22k of *everything*, of
  which advertising is a fraction; a human can watch it.
- **No third-party-advertiser module.** The candidate is statutorily barred from being one
  (s. 88.6(5) para. 1), and building tooling for supporters' independent spending would blur the very
  line s. 88.4 draws.
- **No cross-platform "unified dashboard".** There is one platform.

## 6. What I could not verify, and what to check before spending

Listed plainly, because each one could change a decision above.

1. **The 25 km Special Ad Category radius floor and political ads.** Meta's Marketing API
   documentation says the location restrictions do not apply to `ISSUES_ELECTIONS_POLITICS`; a large
   quantity of agency writing says they do. I trust the primary source, but it is API documentation
   rather than the Ads Manager help centre. **Check it in Ads Manager in ten minutes** (§1.3.1). This
   is the highest-value unknown in the document.
2. **Meta's authorization steps, ID list and timings for Canada.** Meta's own Business Help Center
   pages render as JavaScript shells to a fetcher and I had no logged-in session, so the mailed
   confirmation code, the 3–10 business day letter and the two-to-three-week total are **secondary
   sources** (§1.3). The policy itself, from Meta's Transparency Center, is primary and verified.
3. **Six-character Canadian postal-code targeting.** Confirmed that Meta accepts Canadian postal
   codes; not confirmed that full six-character precision is honoured. Immaterial to the
   recommendation, which is FSA-level (§3.2).
4. **Meta's minimum audience size.** I would not quote a number. You will find it as an ad set that
   refuses to deliver (§3.3).
5. **What fraction of `N0L` and `N0M` is actually inside Middlesex Centre.** The one number that
   determines the true cost per elector, and I do not have it. StatCan will settle it — step 2 of §5.
6. **Whether Meta's authorization flow satisfies MEA s. 88.3(3)'s "in writing to the publisher".**
   A question for counsel. The belt-and-braces workaround in §2.1 costs nothing, so it is moot in
   practice, but do not let anyone tell you the statute is satisfied automatically.
7. **Spotify's direct-sales-only restriction on political inventory.** Secondary sources only;
   Spotify's own policy page states the verification requirement but not the purchase channel
   (§1.2). Does not affect the conclusion — Canada is not an eligible market either way.
8. **Google / YouTube was not in scope and was not researched.** It is the obvious fourth platform
   and the obvious next question, and Google's election-ads verification has historically demanded
   registration identifiers that a municipal campaign may not have. **Do not assume from this
   document that Google works.** If it is wanted, research it properly before budgeting for it.
9. **Nothing here is legal advice.** §2 quotes the statute and the regulation directly, and those
   quotations are accurate as of the e-Laws consolidation read on 2026-09-10. The application of
   s. 88.3(3) to a platform, and the treatment of a supporter's independent ad spend, are exactly
   the questions where being wrong is a compliance-audit problem rather than a refund.
